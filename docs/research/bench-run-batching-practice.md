## Findings

### 1. What 21 CFR itself requires, in the sections that actually govern lab records

§211.192 (Production record review) requires that "[a]ny unexplained discrepancy ... or the failure of a batch or any of its components to meet any of its specifications shall be thoroughly investigated ... The investigation shall extend to other batches of the same drug product and other drug products that may have been associated with the specific failure or discrepancy." The unit of investigation here is explicitly the **batch** (a manufacturing/production concept), not a laboratory "run" or "worksheet." docs/research/bench-run-batching-practice.md is citing the verbatim text as read from the govinfo PDF.

§211.194 (Laboratory records) requires, per sample, all of: "(1) A description of the sample received for testing ... (2) A statement of each method used ... (3) A statement of the weight or measure of sample used for each test ... (4) A complete record of all data secured in the course of each test ... (5) A record of all calculations ... (6) A statement of the results of tests ... (7) The initials or signature of the person who performs each test ... (8) The initials or signature of a second person ..." Every one of these eight sub-clauses is scoped to "the sample" / "each test," singular. **The regulation's own record-keeping vocabulary is per-sample and per-test. It contains no word for a grouping of several samples analysed together** — no "run," "batch of samples," "worksheet," "set," or "sequence" appears anywhere in §211.192 or §211.194.

§211.160(b) is cited (secondhand, via search summary of the eCFR page, not independently verified against a PDF) as requiring scientifically sound specifications, standards, sampling plans, test procedures, and other laboratory control mechanisms, and — per FDA's own OOS guidance quoting it — instrument calibration (§211.160(b)(4)). Nothing in what I could verify of §211.160 introduces a multi-sample grouping concept either.

**Conclusion on this point:** the CGMP text that directly governs what a laboratory record must contain does not require, or even name, a run/batch/worksheet grouping entity. It requires records scoped to one sample and one test at a time.

### 2. FDA's OOS guidance does use "run" and "sequence" vocabulary — but in a specific, narrower sense than "several samples grouped together"

Quoting the May 2022 guidance (page 4, Section III.A, "Responsibility of the Analyst"), verbatim:

> "Certain analytical methods have system suitability requirements, and systems not meeting these requirements should not be used. For example, in chromatographic systems, reference standard solutions may be injected at intervals throughout chromatographic runs to measure drift, noise, and repeatability. If reference standard responses indicate that the system is not functioning properly, all of the data collected during the suspect time period should be properly identified and should not be used."

This is the clearest passage found anywhere in the sources checked that ties a **shared instrumental "run"** to an invalidation that can sweep in more than one sample's data: if the system-suitability check fails partway through a chromatographic run, "all of the data collected during the suspect time period" — which could span the results of several samples injected during that period — is disqualified together. The guidance does not, however, use a noun like "batch of samples" or "worksheet" for the set of samples caught in that window; it describes the exposure in terms of a *time period* of instrument operation, not a named grouping record.

The only other "run" usage (page 9, Section IV.C.1, Averaging) is unrelated to multi-sample batching: "a series of complete tests (full run-throughs of the test procedure), such as assays, are part of the test method" — this is about repeating the *same* test on the *same* sample, and the guidance is explicit that "[t]his determination is considered one test and one result," distinct from "the analysis of different portions from a lot ... and from multiple full analyses of the same homogenous sample." That is, the guidance's "run"/"set" vocabulary in this section governs *replicate measurement of one sample*, not *multiple different samples analysed together*.

I searched the full text of the guidance (via extracted text and pattern search) for "worksheet," "batch of samples," and "sequence" — **none of these three terms occur anywhere in the document.** "Set" occurs twice, both referring to a set of *results* (from one sample's replicates), not a set of *samples*.

### 3. Where FDA's own guidance discusses cross-sample consequences, it is at the batch level, not a laboratory-run level

Throughout Section V ("Concluding the Investigation"), the guidance repeatedly frames disposition decisions in terms of "the batch" or "the lot" — e.g., "the investigation changes from an OOS investigation into a batch failure investigation, which must be extended to other batches or products that may have been associated with the specific failure (§ 211.192)." This mirrors §211.192's own "batch" framing (Finding 1). The vocabulary FDA uses for scope-of-consequence is manufacturing-batch, not laboratory-analytical-run — with the single exception of the system-suitability/chromatographic-run passage quoted in Finding 2, which is the one place a shared instrumental event (not a shared sample-grouping record) can invalidate more than one sample's result at once.

### 4. ICH Q2(R2) defines system suitability testing but does not itself describe a "run" or "sequence" of samples

ICH Q2(R2) (2023-11-30, current Step 4 text) states, in its glossary: "SYSTEM SUITABILITY TEST (SST) — System suitability tests are developed and used to verify that the measurement system and the analytical operations associated with the analytical procedure are fit for the intended purpose and increase the detectability of unacceptable performance. (ICH Q14)" and in its introduction: "As described in ICH Q14, the system suitability test (SST) is an integral part of analytical procedures and is generally established during development as a regular check of performance."

I searched the full text of ICH Q2(R2) for the words "run" and "sequence." "Sequence" occurs only once, in an unrelated context (nucleic-acid sequence similarity search, in Annex 2 on biological assays). "Run" occurs only twice, both in Annex 2, in the context of intra-assay reproducibility of a biological/nucleic-acid assay ("At least three replicates per run at each positive control level in at least 6 runs over two or more days") — describing repeated executions of the *same* assay over time for validation purposes, not multiple different samples grouped into one analytical run during routine testing.

**This is a negative finding worth stating plainly: ICH Q2(R2), despite being the current international guideline on analytical procedure validation and despite explicitly discussing system suitability testing, does not itself use "run," "sequence," "batch," or "worksheet" vocabulary to describe grouping several different samples together for routine analysis.** It establishes that an SST exists as "an integral part of analytical procedures," but does not spell out — in the text I could read — how many samples, if any, must or conventionally do share a single SST bracket. I could not settle, from ICH Q2(R2) alone, whether SST is inherently a multi-sample structure or whether a single-sample test run with its own bracketing SST is equally compliant.

### 5. USP <621> and <711> — second-hand only, not independently verified

I could not reach the USP General Chapters <621> (Chromatography) or <711> (Dissolution) text directly; uspnf.com and usp.org either returned 403 errors or required a paid subscription session I do not have. What follows is reported strictly as **paraphrase of secondary sources found by search**, not as primary-source quotation, per the task's explicit instruction not to paraphrase paywalled text I have not read — I am instead reporting what search-engine summaries of secondary commentary *say* the chapters say, clearly labelled as such:

- Secondary commentary (a chromatography-industry blog and a chromatography forum thread) describes USP <621> system-suitability practice as involving a "bracketing" injection of a reference/system-suitability standard "after a set number of sample injections and following the final sample injection," with acceptance criteria on how much the bracketing injections' response may drift from the initial ones. If accurate, this would describe a chromatographic sequence containing more than one sample injection between SST brackets — i.e., a batched run — but **I have not read the chapter itself to confirm this wording or its status as a requirement versus a common practice.**
- Secondary commentary on USP <711> (a Dissolution Technologies Q&A column and a pharma blog, summarised via search) describes the standard three-stage acceptance testing scheme (S1/S2/S3, or L1/L2/L3) as testing 6 dosage units at S1, an additional 6 at S2 (12 total), and an additional 12 at S3 (24 total), and states that USP dissolution apparatus 1 and 2 are designed for one dosage unit per vessel. **I have not read <711> itself and cannot confirm the exact wording of the acceptance table or the "one unit per vessel" statement against the primary chapter text.** If the "one unit per vessel, six (or more) vessels run together" description is accurate, dissolution testing would be structurally batched by the physical apparatus (multiple units analysed in parallel, in the same run, to reach a single lot disposition decision) in a way that a single titration or pH measurement is not — but this remains an inference from unverified secondary sources, not a sourced fact.

### 6. Which test types batch and which do not — no single regulatory source settles this; it is a matter of apparatus design, not codified requirement

No primary source examined (21 CFR, the OOS guidance, or ICH Q2(R2)) draws an explicit taxonomy dividing "batched" from "one-at-a-time" test types. The closest thing to support for such a distinction is the physical/structural argument from apparatus design (chromatographic system-suitability brackets spanning several injections per Finding 2 and the *unverified* USP <711> multi-vessel description in Finding 5) versus the absence of any comparable shared-run structure for simple wet-chemistry tests (titration, pH, loss on drying, appearance) in any source read. **This is an inference from apparatus mechanics, not a citable regulatory or pharmacopoeial statement that such a taxonomy exists.**

### 7. Shared calibration / standard curve — no explicit multi-sample linkage requirement found

§211.194(c) (read verbatim from the govinfo PDF) requires: "Complete records shall be maintained of any testing and standardization of laboratory reference standards, reagents, and standard solutions." This requires the *standard's own testing and standardisation* to be recorded, but the verbatim text does not say the record must *link* that standard preparation to the list of samples that shared it in one calibration run. I found no passage in 21 CFR, the OOS guidance, or ICH Q2(R2) that explicitly requires such a sample-to-shared-preparation link record. FDA's OOS guidance discusses reference standard responses (system suitability) as I quoted in Finding 2, but frames the consequence as "all of the data collected during the suspect time period," not as a formal cross-reference data structure.

### 9. LIMS/CDS precedent beyond SENAITE

- **SENAITE**: per the existing scout report in this repository (`docs/research/senaite-sample-and-assignment-model.md`), the Worksheet is a first-class, distinctly-typed content object (`Worksheet` portal type, its own ID template `WS-{seq:03d}`) that groups analyses from many different samples via a `UIDReferenceField` back-reference, with its own workflow state and its own `Analyst` field distinct from each individual `Analysis.Analyst`. This is **software design**, not a regulatory requirement — SENAITE's own source code is the authority for this fact, and it does not cite 21 CFR or USP as the reason for the worksheet's existence anywhere in the files that scout examined.
- **OpenChrom**: per its own wiki page (summarised via search, not read directly), OpenChrom supports "batch processing" as a feature for running many chromatogram files through the same processing workflow (an `InputEntries`/`ProcessEntries` batch-file structure) — this is a *data-processing convenience* for applying one method to many files, not necessarily evidence of a "worksheet"-equivalent grouping-of-samples-for-joint-analysis entity in the SENAITE sense. **I did not read the OpenChrom documentation directly and cannot confirm this distinction with full confidence.**
- **Chromeleon, Empower, LabKey**: not reached. No claim made.

**Conclusion on this point:** SENAITE's worksheet is confirmed (by the existing scout, reading the source directly) to be a first-class software grouping entity built for exactly the multi-sample-run scenario this question asks about. I could not find or read a second, independent open-source or documented commercial LIMS/CDS data model in the time available that either corroborates or contradicts this pattern — the search for OpenChrom's model returned only a batch-*processing* feature (applying one workflow to many files), which is not clearly the same concept as SENAITE's worksheet (grouping heterogeneous analyses from many different samples into one unit of assignable lab work).

- Whether §211.160 itself (as opposed to the OOS guidance's summary of it) contains any language bearing on multi-sample grouping — I could not get past a redirect/bot-block to read eCFR's rendering of this section directly.

This question is about laboratory operations vocabulary and structure, which 21 CFR and the OOS guidance are largely silent on beyond the passages quoted above.
